**In plain words.** The Indus Valley civilization, in what is now Pakistan and northwest India, left thousands of short inscriptions on seals and tablets from about 2600 to 1900 BC. The average inscription is about five signs long, there is no bilingual text, and the language is unknown. Some scholars doubt it encodes language at all. We have not proposed a reading. The plan is to test whether sign patterns predict missing signs once copies of the same inscription are not counted twice.

These are questions about the material that can be measured before anyone proposes a reading. Answering all of them would not decipher the script; it would make a later claim testable. Nothing here is a reading, and no probability of decipherment is assigned.

Yadav and colleagues' 2010 primary study discusses a limited corpus, lack of bilingual texts and uncertain underlying language. It tests n-gram prediction and positional regularities. Those are sign-sequence experiments, not a decipherment or a demonstration that a particular proposed language is correct. [Primary n-gram study](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0009506).

Rao's 2018 authored chapter proposes economic uses for particular seals and tablets. Its rationing interpretation is a hypothesis based on selected archaeological comparisons, not a supplied translation or a settled explanation of every inscription. [Economic-use hypothesis](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.00049).

## Corpus and limits

The statistical paper identifies its concordance and data selection. Sign inventories and corpus versions must therefore be named when reproducing it; counts from different classifications cannot be silently combined. The paper was inspected, but no complete openly reusable machine-readable concordance was acquired and verified here. A paper containing corpus examples does not establish bulk-data access. [Study methods](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0009506).

An intake should retain artifact ID, site, object type, inventory/version, original sign IDs, missing or disputed signs, physical orientation and whether the image is a seal face or an impression. Where an inscription is repeated on related artifacts, record that dependency instead of treating every copy as independent evidence.

## Proposed next experiment, not executed here

1. Obtain a documented, lawfully reusable concordance or a bounded hand-checked sample of at most 500 inscriptions. Create an explicit crosswalk when comparing two sign inventories. Mark unresolved correspondences rather than guessing them.
2. Group duplicate impressions and related artifacts before reserving 20% of object groups. Compare n-grams of orders 1 through 4 with unigram and start/end-position baselines. Predeclare at most 2,000 length-preserving shuffles per statistic and fixed seeds.
3. Mask only clearly read held-out signs, separately reporting damaged or uncertain positions. Report predictive log loss and exact sign accuracy, stratified by length and object type. Repeat with an explicitly declared orientation variant; do not reverse strings to maximize a result.

This protocol could show that a model predicts unseen signs better than a simple baseline. It would not by itself distinguish all linguistic and nonlinguistic generative systems, name the language or recover word meanings.

## Evidence standard

A translation requires consistent sign interpretation on independent material and external linguistic or archaeological constraints. Repeated-sign structure, entropy resemblance and a successful reconstruction of a missing symbol are weaker claims. Report both successful and failed comparisons across inventories. If no adequate independent test set exists, the result remains limited rather than being promoted to a reading.

The first actionable milestone is a versioned corpus and dependency-aware baseline, not a search for attractive modern-language words in an assumed Roman alphabet.
