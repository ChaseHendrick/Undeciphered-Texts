# What others have tried on the 1939 challenge, and how close anyone is

Read on 5 October 2026. This extends [the 4 October note](dagapeyeff-others-2026-10-04.md). Pages were fetched directly where the network allowed, and otherwise read through search summaries; each source says which. Quoted readings are other people's claims, quoted so they can be checked. None of them is a reading, and none is claimed here.

## How close is anyone

No one is close. No published work gives a key and a plaintext that encrypt back to the 392 digits and read as language. No two independent searches have converged on even a partial reading. Every serious search that ran a fair control found its best score matched or beaten by shuffled or synthetic cells. Every published reading checked below fails a count check the cells force, imposes its own answer, or uses too few of the cells to be tested.

What the serious work agrees on:

1. The digits are 196 Polybius pairs, rows from 67890 and columns from 12345, then 000. Every careful source finds this.
2. The rarest symbols sit in the last column of a 14 by 14 layout (Pelling, January 2014; quantified by Snider, 2026, and here).
3. Plain substitution, and the plainest transpositions with a substitution, do not read. Gariazzo (2026) and this repository both ran these with fair controls. This repository also showed the search can find planted texts at widths 1, 2, 4 and 7.
4. The large-key hypotheses (a 14-column key, a turning grille, double transposition) have not been searched with demonstrated power. Tiago Rodrigues (2014) needed about 240 letters for a 14-column key with a substitution. Here, the joint search recovered 0 of 3 planted width-14 texts and 0 of 2 planted grilles. Gariazzo checked key recovery only for four-square, at 83 percent. A "no signal" for these families closes nothing yet.
5. A construction with no message reproduces every measured statistic (van Eykelen, MsgTrail, May and June 2026). That does not show there is no message. It does show the statistics cannot show there is one.
6. D'Agapeyeff made enciphering errors in his own worked example: 89 pairs for 92 letters, and AREA written as ARYA. That is about 4 faults in 92 letters. At that rate the challenge would carry about 8.

## The primary record

- Alexander d'Agapeyeff, *Codes and Ciphers*, Oxford University Press, 1939, first edition only. Page numbers differ between sources (144, 158 and 159 are all cited). The challenge was dropped from later editions.
- David Shulman (as Ab Struse), *The Cryptogram*, April/May 1952, 39-40 and 46, and March/April 1959, 80-81. Wayne G. Barker, "The Unsolved D'Agapeyeff Cipher", *Cryptologia* 2(2), 1978, 144-147. Neither was opened here.
- That he forgot the method is reported as "said to have admitted" (Wikipedia). Billy Snider's repository (2026) notes that no primary source for it has been located. Treat it as lore.
- [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%27Agapeyeff_cipher), fetched. It prints the worked Polybius exercise as 178 characters and its square as SDUMI, FWAOY, VN*TE, LHRCQ, BPK** with I and J together. That is the square `engine.dagapeyeff_booksquare` rebuilt from the alignment, with the same three cells unused. Wikipedia marks ARYA for AREA. It quotes page 111 on dummies: every third, fourth or fifth letter. It also notes two shorter exercises, about 100 letters each.
- Gordon Rugg and Gavin Taylor, Hyde and Rugg, "A very British mystery", parts 1 to 5, July and August 2013 (search summaries). Taylor bought a first edition and found a proofreading error in the book's list of Valerio's letters. Rugg worked with Robert Matthews, Stephen Antrobus and Taylor on the cipher.
- [Nick Pelling, 5 March 2017](https://ciphermysteries.com/2017/03/05/new-clue-dagapeyeff-challenge-cipher), fetched. Following a reader named Marie: the book's double transposition is copied from Kerckhoffs, *La Cryptographie Militaire* (1883). The book prints the key as SCHUVALOF; Kerckhoffs has SCHUVALOW. Both number the columns in the decryption direction; for REUNIONTO the encryption numbering is 623781459. The example is a 9 by 9 square with the nulls abcd. Pelling suspects d'Agapeyeff thought double transposition undid itself. If the challenge used that method, he could not have decrypted it.

## Searches with controls

- **Ruben Gariazzo**, [project page](https://ruben-gariazzo.fr/en/projects/agapeyeff-cryptanalysis/) and report v2, [doi:10.5281/zenodo.22057249](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.22057249), 22 August 2026, both fetched. Code and data: [doi:10.5281/zenodo.21970478](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21970478), a 711 MB archive, not downloaded. His first solver used an 8,000-character trigram model (the Declaration of Independence), and authentic Austen scored below its threshold. That is the same failure this repository found in its own 8,689-letter model today. He rebuilt it on 2.5 to 3.29 million characters of quadgrams and searched substitution on 20 fixed routes, a free column order, a 14 by 14 Fleissner grille, and double transposition at free widths. He used nine transcription variants, three languages and a shuffled-cell baseline at equal volume, across about 5.97 million structural combinations and 3,753,383 logged attempts. The baseline's best, -860.37, beat the cells' best, -877.84, and both beat authentic English of that length (-935 to -1,160). The most coherent phrase in the whole project came from a shuffled sequence: "HELD IF WE CAN WIN". Four-square reached 20.9 percent coverage after tuning to 83 percent recovery of known keys. His "100 percent coverage" counts structural combinations, not shown recovery.
- **Erik van Eykelen**, MsgTrail, parts I to XV, March to June 2026; [XIV](https://msgtrail.com/posts/unmasking-the-dagapeyeff-cipher-xiv-pessimistic-take) and [XV](https://msgtrail.com/posts/unmasking-the-dagapeyeff-cipher-xv-the-recipe-not-the-message) fetched. Part XIV: letters drawn at the cells' own 18 frequencies, with the five rare pairs forced into the last column, reach the cells' annealing scores; the real cells' best was -12.75 a character, and synthetic streams reached -12.23 to -12.61. Three near-best decodes of the real cells share no word of five letters or more. Part XV: of 720 procedures a person could do by hand, tallying 18 pairs and dealing them onto the grid, moving a rare pair to the row end, matches all 14 fingerprint statistics. One trace survives every model: symbols three apart are slightly correlated. Uneven widths 2 to 13 were then attacked against 60 fakes, and the real cells did no better. The best real decode used seven letters, "SEESTREEHASH". He concludes the cipher is empty, and says statistics alone cannot decide.
- **Tim Marland**, [dagapeyeffresearch.com](https://dagapeyeffresearch.com/), 2026, home page fetched once; the site asks not to be scraped. Seven phases, six complete: 212 configurations, then more than 280 runs over 7 methods. An ADFGX period-7 spike was later called an overfitting artefact. 5,040 column orders all scored alike. Two-square reached the project's best score. Esperanto was rejected. 116 cribs from the book and 42 keyword sweeps all failed. Pair 04 at position 97 is flagged as interesting but inconclusive. He reports George Lasry as having ruled out simple substitution plus transposition; no publication by Lasry on this was located.
- **Tiago Rodrigues** and **Jim Melichar**, comments on [Pelling, 23 December 2013](https://ciphermysteries.com/2013/12/23/dagapeyeff-cipher), May and June 2014, fetched. Rodrigues: a transposition-first, then substitution solver needed about 240 letters for a 14-column key and failed on 196-letter samples. He noted 04 sits at the exact center of a 28 by 7 layout. Melichar: searched many books, billions of windows, for a 196-letter passage with IC at least 0.069, at least 7 unused letters, and at least 22 for the 11th and 12th letters together; he found none.
- **Billy Snider**, [dagapeyeff-col14](https://github.com/billysnider-collab/dagapeyeff-col14), 2026, README fetched. Confirms the last-column concentration at about 9e-10 and reports four failed readings of that column (date, coordinate, checksum, position).

## Published readings, quoted and checked

The checks are in `engine.dagapeyeff_claims` and its test. If every cell is a letter, a one-to-one key and any transposition keep the cells' sorted counts: 20, 17, 17, 17, 17, 16, 15, 14, 12, 12, 11, 11, 9, 3, 2, 1, 1, 1. A reading that drops cells as dummies can still use at most 18 letters. The book's own dummy rule keeps 131 to 157 cells.

## What would move it

Not another score on the same 196 cells. Something from outside: d'Agapeyeff's papers or letters, the 1952 and 1959 *Cryptogram* notes if they report anything from him, a second message in the same system, or a crib with a source. Inside the cells, the one open technical job is a search that recovers planted 14-column, grille or double-transposition texts with an unknown key, with errors planted at the book's own rate, before it is run on the cells.

Update, 6 October 2026: the 14-column part is done. A compiled joint search recovered 18 of 18 planted texts, 6 with 8 wrong cells, and the cells stayed inside their shuffles ([note](dagapeyeff-columnar14c-2026-10-06.md)). The compiled grille search still finds a planted grille only with the key given ([note](dagapeyeff-grillec-2026-10-06.md)).

## Added 6 October 2026

A second search found sources the survey above did not list. None gives a checkable full-length reading.

- **David Shulman** (as Ab Struse), "The D'Agapeyeff Cryptogram: A Challenge", *The Cryptogram*, April/May 1952. Title from search results; not opened.
- **Klaus Schmeh**, [MysteryTwister challenge write-up](https://mysterytwister.org/media/challenges/pdf/mtc3-schmeh-02-agapeyeff-en.pdf), listing the cipher as an open challenge. Read through search results.
- **NumberWorld blog**, [part 1](https://numberworld.blogspot.com/2013/07/dagapeyeff-cipher-part-1.html) (2013) and [part 2](http://numberworld.blogspot.com/2015/07/d-cipher-part-2.html) (2015). Considers the book itself as the key of a dictionary code, and notes that the cryptogram was still printed in a 1949 reprint and first left out in 1952. Read through search summaries.
- **Please Decipher Me blog**, [2010](http://pleasedecipherme.blogspot.com/2010/11/is-dagapeyeff-cipher-adfgx-cipher.html) and [2011](http://pleasedecipherme.blogspot.com/2011/01/dagapeyeff-cipher-searching-for.html): an ADFGX hypothesis and a search for a solution. Read through search summaries.
- **Ashley and Antigravity**, [dagapeyeff-cipher-solver](https://github.com/ajejfiejof/dagapeyeff-cipher-solver), 2026, commit `2009392`, README and PAPER.md read. It argues from the index of coincidence (0.0697) that the cipher is a "mathematically pure" monoalphabetic Polybius substitution on a 14 by 14 matrix, with `04` marking a midpoint. No plaintext is given. The index is unchanged by any one-to-one key, so it cannot show monoalphabeticity; the cells' letter counts, which such a key keeps, are at least 8 errors from any of 479,051 English windows ([errors](dagapeyeff-errors-2026-10-06.md)). The `04` midpoint is Rodrigues's 2014 observation.
- Craig Bauer's *Unsolved!* (Princeton, 2017) may discuss the cipher; that could not be confirmed here.

No letter string from this repository's searches is stored.
